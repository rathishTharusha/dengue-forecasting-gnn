\subsection{No Configuration Clears the Persistence Floor}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports rolling-origin test performance. A naive persistence
forecast achieves RMSE $55.12$, and every graph configuration \emph{we} tested is
worse. We are explicit about that scope: these runs evaluate the architectures of
Section~\ref{sec:framework}, not the published spatio-temporal models. Those are
addressed in Section~\ref{sec:reconcile} using their authors' own numbers and
protocol. The gap is not a rounding artefact: against the adaptive-graph model a paired sign
test over 64 matched runs favours persistence at $p = 0.008$, and against the best
regularised variant at $p = 0.033$.

One model is the exception to the significance, though not to the ordering. With a
gated temporal convolution the model reaches $56.78$ and is the only configuration
whose difference from persistence is \emph{not} statistically detectable
($26/64$, $p = 0.169$). It does not beat the floor; it is the first model that
cannot be shown to be worse than it.

We report this as the paper's central finding. Persistence is a strong competitor
here for a structural reason --- the lag-1 autocorrelation of the target is $0.68$
--- and prior work on this dataset does not test against it. A spatio-temporal GNN
that loses to a naive forecast is a materially different conclusion from one that
beats traditional early-warning systems, and both can be true of the same model
under different protocols.

\subsection{Comparison with Non-Graph Methods}
Table~\ref{tab:main} also reports every competitor re-run under our protocol
rather than cited from an incommensurable one. The ordering is uncomfortable for
the premise of this work.

A random forest over pooled district-weeks achieves $56.31$ --- better than every
graph configuration we tested, including the best --- and is not significantly
worse than persistence ($9/24$, $p = 0.31$). XGBoost reaches $57.41$ and a
graph-free LSTM $57.95$, both also ahead of the adaptive graph at $61.81$.

The LSTM comparison is the informative one, because it differs from the graph
models in essentially one respect: it has no adjacency. That it outperforms them
indicates the graph structure is not contributing on this benchmark. Two naive
methods bracket the range: seasonal naive is far worse than persistence
($153.17$; $0/8$, $p = 0.008$), so dengue's year-over-year amplitude varies too
much for a 52-week lag, and ridge regression is unstable rather than merely poor
--- a single fold reaches RMSE $1959$ because linear extrapolation in
log-residual space passes through an exponential inverse transform. Its mean is
not a meaningful summary and we report it only to document the failure mode.

\subsection{The Temporal Operator, Not the Graph}
The largest single effect we measured is architectural. Adding the gated dilated
causal convolution of Graph WaveNet~\cite{wu2019graphwavenet} improves RMSE from
$61.81$ to $56.78$: $46/64$ paired wins, $p = 0.0006$.

This corrects an omission in our own earlier design rather than introducing a new
component. The model previously flattened the $W \times F$ input window into a
single vector and applied a linear map, so despite being described as
spatio-temporal it contained no temporal operator at all --- while every method it
was benchmarked against (DCRNN~\cite{li2018dcrnn}, EpiGNN~\cite{xie2022epignn},
Cola-GNN~\cite{deng2020colagnn}) carries one. The measured consequence is that a
graph-free LSTM outperformed the graph models. No amount of hyper-parameter search
addresses a missing component.

\subsection{The Learned Adjacency Does Not Replicate}
At three seeds the learned adjacency beat its matched fixed-graph control on
$17/24$ paired runs ($p = 0.064$), which we initially read as a promising
under-powered effect. A paired power analysis on those same runs indicated
$n \approx 54$ was required. At eight seeds ($n = 64$) the comparison is
$35/64$, $p = 0.53$ --- indistinguishable from chance.

The mean still improves by $2.78$ RMSE, but only a minority of runs improve, so
the mean is carried by a few large gains. We therefore do not claim the adaptive
graph as a contribution. We report it as an effect that appeared at small $n$ and
disappeared under replication, which is the outcome the replication was run to
detect.

\subsection{A Generation-Interval Constraint Replicates}
By contrast, constraining the second difference of predicted $\log(1+y)$ ---
derived from dengue's $\sim$3-week generation interval, and computable without any
latent compartment --- improves on the unconstrained model at $46/64$,
$p = 0.0006$. It is the only mechanistic component we tested that survives
replication.

Two failures around it are worth reporting. A curriculum that ramps the constraint
weight, following Krishnapriyan et al.~\cite{krishnapriyan2021failure}, made
results \emph{worse} at eight seeds ($+2.54$ RMSE) despite appearing to help at
three. And a spatial-smoothness penalty, which is minimised by a more uniform
graph, works against the learned adjacency and degrades accuracy at every weight
tested.

\subsection{Shrinkage Toward the Naive Forecast}
Because the network predicts a residual over persistence, scaling that residual by
$\gamma \in [0,1]$ interpolates between the naive forecast and the model.
Selecting $\gamma$ on the validation fold improves RMSE overall ($p = 0.028$).

The selected values are themselves informative. For the weak adaptive model
validation chooses $\bar{\gamma} = 0.56$, discarding nearly half the predicted
correction; for the stronger temporal model it chooses $\bar{\gamma} = 0.82$. The
procedure asks for less shrinkage precisely when the model carries more signal,
and consequently shrinkage \emph{harms} the temporal model ($56.78 \to 57.74$).

\subsection{Augmentation Does Not Help, and the GAN Hurts}

  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
    \toprule
    Augmentation & RMSE & vs.\ window warp \\
    \midrule
    None                      & $\mathbf{61.81}$ & --- \\
    Window warping            & $66.60$ & --- \\

    Conditional WGAN-GP       & $93.81$ & $4/24$, $p = 0.0015$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:augment} reports the third proposed contribution. A conditional
WGAN-GP over $(\text{window} \rightarrow \text{horizon})$ pairs is significantly
worse than window warping ($p = 0.0015$) and than jittering ($p = 0.023$).

The stronger finding is that \emph{no} augmentation helps: every augmented arm is
worse than training on the real data alone. We evaluated the generator on
downstream forecast accuracy rather than on distributional similarity to real
series, which is the criterion under which a generator can appear successful while
contributing nothing.

\subsection{Reconciliation with Reported Results on This Dataset}
\label{sec:reconcile}
Weng et al.~\cite{weng2024graph} report spatio-temporal GNNs outperforming their
baselines on this dataset, which appears to contradict the results above. It does
not. The two sets of figures are computed with different aggregations.

We reimplemented their protocol --- rolling cross-validation over five nested
segments at 60--100\% of the series, each split $70/30$ --- and computed a
persistence forecast on it three ways.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{Persistence RMSE on the protocol of Weng et al., under three
  aggregations of the same predictions.}
  \label{tab:aggregation}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lc@{}}
    \toprule
    Aggregation & Persistence RMSE \\
    \midrule
    Pooled over all district-weeks & 58.79 \\
    Mean of per-district RMSEs     & 44.59 \\
    Mean of per-window RMSEs       & \textbf{38.46} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:aggregation} spans a factor of $1.53$ from a reporting choice
alone. On a target with median 13 and maximum 2{,}631 a handful of outbreak
windows dominate a pooled RMSE, whereas averaging per-window RMSEs weights every
window equally regardless of magnitude; since the square root is concave, the
latter is systematically smaller.

Their released implementation resolves which applies. It evaluates with
\texttt{batch\_size = 1} and accumulates the RMSE of each batch before dividing by
the batch count, so the published values are means of per-window RMSEs.

A second choice compounds it, and their code settles that too. Every
\texttt{run\_*} function computes the held-out score and discards it, reporting
instead the loader over the whole segment --- so Table~I's ``Cross Validated''
column is \emph{training-inclusive}, with roughly $70\%$ of each reported
evaluation set seen during training, while its ``Full Dataset'' column is the
genuinely held-out score. The column names invert the usual reading. Recomputing
persistence under their aggregation on each basis gives $34.67$ against the
cross-validated column and $39.08$ against the full-dataset column.

On the matching basis every model in their Table~I --- from STGAT at $44.78$ to
ARIMA at $189.22$ --- is worse than a naive forecast. Persistence is not among the
baselines they report. We have since run their released code unmodified at a
pinned commit: all five graph architectures reproduce, four of them within
$3.4\%$ and AAGCN to $0.002\%$, so this comparison rests on a reproduction rather
than on their printed table alone.

Two conclusions follow. Our finding is not in tension with the published result;
it is the same phenomenon, visible once a naive baseline is included. And absolute
RMSE is not portable between papers that aggregate differently, so we state our
own aggregation --- pooled within fold, averaged across folds --- wherever a
number appears.

\subsection{Peak Timing}
\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{Peak week error: mean absolute displacement, in weeks, between observed
  and forecast outbreak peak.}
  \label{tab:horizon}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
    \toprule
    Model & $h{=}1$ & $h{=}2$ & $h{=}3$ \\
    \midrule
    Persistence floor            & $\mathbf{1.70}$ & $\mathbf{2.73}$ & $\mathbf{4.02}$ \\
    + growth constraint, shrunk  & 2.08 & 3.26 & 4.63 \\
    + gated temporal conv.       & 2.20 & 3.37 & 4.64 \\
    adaptive graph (no temporal) & 2.54 & 3.74 & 4.85 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Squared error is not what an early-warning system is judged on. Measuring instead
where each model places the outbreak peak (Table~\ref{tab:horizon}) separates the
configurations in the same order but exposes a gap that RMSE understates: every
model calls the peak later than persistence does, by roughly half a week at
$h{=}1$ and by a similar margin throughout.

Persistence places the peak within one week at $h{=}1$ and degrades by about a
week per horizon step, which is exactly what a forecast shifted $h$ weeks late must
do and confirms the measurement is sound. A model whose squared error approaches
persistence but which calls the peak later cannot support pre-positioning, and
reporting aggregate error alone would conceal this.
