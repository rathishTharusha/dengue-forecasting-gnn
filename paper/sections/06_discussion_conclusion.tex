\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\cB{\textbf{When does a mechanistic prior pay?} Physics-informed networks succeed
where data are scarce relative to the dynamics: MP-PINN~\cite{nguyen2024mppinn}
trains on 35 days and forecasts 97, a regime in which a purely data-driven model
has almost nothing to learn from. Our setting is the opposite. The decisive
quantity is not the amount of data but how much of the target a naive baseline
already explains. Here the previous week explains $r^2=0.85$; the mechanistic
decomposition divides that out and leaves $R_t$, which is 26\% predictable.
A prior helps when it supplies structure the data cannot; it hurts when it
routes a well-determined quantity through a poorly-determined one. The ratio of
those two predictabilities, not the sample size, is the criterion we would apply
before attempting this again.}

\cB{\textbf{Negative results with a mechanism.} Five separate physics
formulations were tried across this project and all failed. Only the last is
informative, because it is accompanied by the measurement that makes failure
inevitable rather than by a hyper-parameter that was not tuned enough. We would
argue this is the reporting standard such results need: a physics term that fails
at one weighting is a tuning anecdote, while one that cannot succeed at any
weighting because the quantity it depends on carries a $1.90\times$ error is a
finding.}

\cC{\textbf{Implications for benchmarking.} Three practices would have changed the
published conclusions on this dataset, and none is expensive: report a naive
baseline, pool squared error rather than averaging per window, and inspect the
test split for administrative artifacts before reporting fold-level results. The
last is the least standard and, here, the largest---it moves the floor by 15 RMSE
and inverts which fold looks hardest.}

\cD{\textbf{Limitations.} Our conclusions concern one country, one disease and one
processed array; the artifact analysis in particular is dataset-specific, though
the practice it motivates is not. The adaptive-graph comparison after the
self-path correction rests on $n=9$ and is reported as unsettled. We did not
evaluate a GAN, and the augmentation contribution the project originally proposed
is not tested here. Finally, the detection result uses a threshold definition of
an outbreak; 309 episodes exist in the record but the median episode lasts one
week, so a sustained-exceedance definition would be more operationally meaningful
and is what we would adopt next.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\cE{We reproduced five published spatio-temporal GNNs on Sri Lankan dengue
surveillance to within 7.3\%, and showed that their reported advantage rests on
training-inclusive, per-window-averaged evaluation without a naive baseline.
Re-evaluated under a leak-free rolling-origin protocol with artifact-aware dual
scoring, no architecture and no evidence-led increment separates from a
persistence floor of 29.52 at $p<0.05$.}

\cE{We then tested the mechanistic prior the task invites and found it cannot
help, for a reason we can state as a number: the reproduction number is 26\%
predictable, so reconstructing a forecast through it costs a $1.90\times$
multiplicative error per step where persistence costs none. The ceiling on this
task is informational, not architectural---predicted growth carries no information
about realised growth, and under squared error that is the optimal response.}

\cF{The same mechanistic quantity is, however, the largest single contributor to a
target that \emph{is} tractable: ranking which districts will breach an alert
threshold three weeks ahead, where it lifts AUC from 0.807 to 0.826. We suggest
that is where mechanistic structure belongs on endemic, strongly autocorrelated
surveillance data---in state estimation and early warning rather than in point
forecasting. All code, data, figures and the notebook that regenerates every
number in this paper are available in the project repository.}
