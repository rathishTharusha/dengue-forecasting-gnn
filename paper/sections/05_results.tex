\subsection{The Persistence Floor Is Not Cleared}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports rolling-origin test performance, and its first row
frames everything else: naive persistence achieves RMSE $44.80$, and no graph
configuration we tested beats it. The fixed-graph model reaches $45.47$, the
adaptive-graph model $45.00$.

The margins are small against the spread. Fold RMSE ranges from roughly 27 to 68
with the epidemic regime of the test window, a standard deviation of $\pm16$ to
$\pm21$ against differences of half a point. A paired sign test over matched
$(\text{fold}, \text{seed})$ runs avoids relying on that comparison and says the
same: the fixed-graph model beats persistence on four of nine runs, the adaptive
model on three. Neither is distinguishable from chance at this sample size.

This is a finding, not a shortfall. Persistence is strong here for a structural
reason --- lag-1 autocorrelation of the target is $0.68$ --- and prior work on
this dataset never tests against it. A spatio-temporal GNN that merely matches a
naive forecast is a materially different conclusion from one that beats
traditional early-warning systems, and both can hold of the same model.

\subsection{The Learned Graph Helps; the Regulariser Does Not}
Against its matched control the learned adjacency improves RMSE from $45.47$ to
$45.00$ and SMAPE from $69.6\%$ to $69.0\%$. Control and treatment share the
propagation operator, head, optimiser and target handling, differing only in
whether $\Ablend$ is gated, so the difference is attributable to the graph. The
gate itself settles at $\sigma(g) = 0.62$ from an initialisation of $0.82$:
the model moves toward the learned structure while retaining substantial weight
on geography, which is what the gate was introduced to permit.

Spatial regularisation does not help at any weight (Table~\ref{tab:lambda}).
Every non-zero $\lambda$ is worse than none and $\lambda = 1.0$ more than doubles
MAE. The gate rises toward geography as $\lambda$ grows, reaching $0.95$ at
$\lambda = 0.1$: a smoothness penalty is minimised by a more uniform graph, so it
pulls against the structure the adaptive component is learning. The two
contributions are not merely independent but mildly antagonistic.

\subsection{Peak Timing Is the Larger Gap}
Squared error is not what an early-warning system is judged on. Measuring instead
where each model places the outbreak peak (Table~\ref{tab:horizon}) separates the
models far more sharply.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{Peak week error: mean absolute displacement, in weeks, between
  observed and forecast outbreak peak. Lower is better.}
  \label{tab:horizon}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
    \toprule
    Model & $h{=}1$ & $h{=}2$ & $h{=}3$ \\
    \midrule
    Persistence floor      & \textbf{0.99} & \textbf{1.90} & \textbf{3.44} \\
    Dense GCN, fixed graph & 1.98 & 4.59 & 5.47 \\
    \quad + adaptive graph & 2.92 & 4.70 & 6.56 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Persistence places the peak within a week at $h{=}1$ and degrades by roughly a
week per horizon step --- exactly what a forecast shifted $h$ weeks late must do,
which confirms the measurement is sound. Both graph models are two to three weeks
worse at every horizon, and the adaptive graph is worse than the fixed one
despite its lower RMSE. A model whose squared error is competitive but which
calls the peak three weeks late cannot support pre-positioning, and the two
properties are evidently not interchangeable. Reporting aggregate error alone
would have concealed this.

Figure~\ref{fig:forecast} shows the same behaviour for Colombo: the broad
epidemic shape is tracked, but onset is answered late. We make no claim that
regularisation suppresses spurious spikes --- the regularised runs are less
accurate overall, and we have no measurement isolating spike suppression from
that general degradation.
