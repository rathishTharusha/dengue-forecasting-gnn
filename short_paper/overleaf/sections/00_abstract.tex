% ABSTRACT

\begin{abstract}
Early warning asks \emph{when} an epidemic arrives, not only how many cases
follow. We show these are different problems on district-level dengue, and that
mechanistic structure answers the first while data answers the second.
Spatio-temporal graph neural networks are widely applied here, yet their advantage
over naive baselines is rarely verified under a leak-free protocol. We reproduce five published ST-GNNs on Sri Lanka's
dengue record (25 districts, 459 weeks) to within 7.3\%, and find the reported
gains are measured with training data included: naive persistence beats every
model in the column the source paper reports. We also identify an administrative
reporting backlog occupying one fold's test split and carrying $\sim$90\% of its
squared error; scoring with and without those windows moves the persistence floor
from RMSE 44.80 to 29.52 and erases the best architecture's 3.8-point lead.
Against that floor, no architecture and none of four evidence-led increments
separate from their own baseline at $p<0.05$. Testing the mechanistic prior the
task invites---a renewal equation whose generation interval comes from a validated
SEIR--SEI model---it reconstructs the record given oracle $R_t$ (RMSE 13.44) and
fails as a forecaster, because $R_t$ is only 26\% predictable, so routing a
forecast through it re-injects a 1.90$\times$ error per step where persistence
carries none. The ceiling is informational, not architectural. The mechanism is
nonetheless useful in the two roles that do not ask it to predict: as an
inequality constraint it reliably improves a model ($-0.044$ RMSE on 57/60 paired
runs, $p<0.0001$), and it is the largest single contributor to ranking which
districts will breach an alert threshold (AUC $0.807 \rightarrow 0.826$).
Mechanistic knowledge pays here when it restricts the hypothesis space, not when
it supplies a forecast.
\end{abstract}
