\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset}
We evaluate on Sri Lanka's national dengue surveillance record, $N=25$
administrative districts over $T=459$ consecutive weeks (2013--2022). Weekly case
counts come from the Ministry of Health Epidemiology Unit; ten environmental
covariates (precipitation, air and land-surface temperature, humidity, NDVI, soil
moisture, canopy cover) are drawn from NASA EarthData. Covariates are pre-shifted
by their empirically optimal lags --- precipitation $\sim$12 weeks, NDVI
$\sim$17 weeks --- reflecting vector breeding and infection-to-diagnosis
intervals. The target is strongly right-skewed: median 13 weekly cases, maximum
2{,}631, and $9.7\%$ of district-weeks zero.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
Models are evaluated under leakage-free rolling-origin (expanding window)
cross-validation across \textbf{eight} chronological origins
($a \in \{0.400, 0.475, \ldots, 0.925\}$, spaced $0.075$) and three or eight
seeds. Each origin's test window is the following $7.5\%$ of the series, so
consecutive test windows are \emph{disjoint} rather than overlapping.

That choice is deliberate. Overlapping windows inflate the apparent sample size:
eight folds sharing more than half their test data are not eight independent
observations, and a paired test over them is anti-conservative. We trade a
shorter test window ($\sim$34 weeks) for folds a paired test can legitimately
treat as independent. Standardisation statistics are computed strictly from
training-fold data, and each run makes one optimiser step per training window.

A rolling-origin protocol is necessary rather than stylistic. Under a single
$70/10/20$ chronological split we measured validation and test RMSE to be
anti-correlated ($r = -0.74$): the two periods fall in different epidemic
regimes, so selecting on one actively mis-selects for the other.

\paragraph{Reporting and statistical power.}
Results are mean $\pm$ standard deviation over matched runs. RMSE is pooled
across horizons within a fold and then averaged across folds; we state this
because pooling and averaging per-horizon RMSE differ by roughly $0.8$ here,
larger than several of the effects discussed. Comparisons use a two-sided paired
sign test over matched $(\text{fold}, \text{seed})$ runs rather than a comparison
of means.

Power is the binding constraint on this dataset and we report it explicitly. The
paired standard deviation of the adaptive-graph effect is $12.0$ RMSE against a
mean effect of $-4.6$, which requires $n \approx 54$ for $80\%$ power; the
mechanistic-constraint effect ($-2.1$, SD $4.8$) requires $n \approx 44$. Results
at smaller $n$ are reported with their $p$-values and described as
under-powered rather than as null.

\subsection{Metrics}
Point accuracy is measured by RMSE, MAE and SMAPE on the original case scale;
SMAPE is the primary percentage metric because frequent zero-case weeks make
plain MAPE unusable.

We additionally report \emph{peak week error}: per district, the absolute
difference in weeks between the observed and forecast week of maximum incidence,
averaged over districts whose observed maximum reaches ten cases. Below that
threshold there is no outbreak to time and the argmax is noise. This is what an
early-warning system is judged on, and it is not recoverable from RMSE. As a
check that the metric measures what it claims, a persistence forecast at horizon
$h$ is shifted $h$ weeks late by construction and must score approximately $h$;
it does (Table~\ref{tab:horizon}).

Following the requirement to characterise cost as well as accuracy, we record
trainable parameter count, training wall-clock per fold and inference latency per
25-district forward pass for every configuration, measured during the run rather
than estimated afterwards.

\subsection{Baselines}
Rather than cite numbers from an incommensurable protocol, we re-run every
competitor under our own.

\textbf{Naive.} Persistence ($\hat{y}_{t+h} = y_t$) and seasonal naive
($\hat{y}_{t+h} = y_{t+h-52}$), both deterministic.
\textbf{Statistical and tabular.} Ridge regression, random forest and
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} over pooled district-weeks.
\textbf{Deep, without a graph.} An LSTM over the same window. This is the control
that isolates what the \emph{graph} contributes as distinct from what a deep
model contributes.
\textbf{Graph.} The dense fixed-adjacency GCN of Section~\ref{sec:framework},
which shares \modelname's propagation path and differs only in whether the
adjacency is learned.

Every baseline consumes the identical windows, target transformation and inverse
transform as the graph models, so differences are attributable to the method and
not to preprocessing. We do not compare numerically against Weng et
al.~\cite{weng2024graph}: their static $70/30$ split and different target
normalisation make the numbers incommensurable.
