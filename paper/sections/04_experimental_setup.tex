\section{Reproducing the Benchmark}
\label{sec:repro}

\cE{We ran the reference implementation~\cite{weng2024graph} unmodified---their
code, their data, their protocol---on a pinned software stack, and recovered their
published numbers to within \textbf{7.3\%} across twenty comparisons, with AAGCN
and DCRNN matching to four decimal places. Three deviations from their environment
were forced and are documented with the exact error that forces each; none changes
a reported figure. The published results are real.}

\cE{What they measure is a different question, and three properties of the
reference evaluation account for the entire apparent advantage.}

\paragraph{The reported column includes training data.}
\cE{The column labelled ``cross validated'' is computed on a loader that spans the
full series rather than a held-out split. It is a fit statistic, not a forecast
statistic, and the column names are effectively inverted relative to their
meanings.}

\paragraph{RMSE is averaged per window, not pooled.}
\cF{Averaging per-window RMSE before aggregating is not the same as pooling
squared error, and by Jensen's inequality it is systematically lower. On this
target the gap is $\sim$39\%.}

\paragraph{The naive baseline is absent.}
\cF{Adding persistence to their own reported column reverses the result: it beats
\emph{every} model there, on both MAE and RMSE. An independent
re-implementation---sharing no code with either the reference or our own
library---reproduces this conclusion, so it is not an artefact of one
codebase.}

\cF{Consequently, published ST-GNN advantages on this benchmark do not survive
contact with a naive baseline evaluated on held-out data. For the remainder of the
paper we take only the \emph{architectures} from the reference work and evaluate
them under the protocol of \S\ref{sec:setup}. Numbers below are therefore not
comparable to the source table, and are comparable to each other.}
