# External review of the 7-page draft (pasted verbatim by the project owner)

Yes. I went through the full 7-page draft carefully. The biggest problems are not grammar or IEEE formatting. The main risks are in the experimental design, strength of the claims, and consistency of the evidence. A reviewer could challenge several conclusions even though the experiments themselves contain useful results.

## Major issues I would fix before submission

| Priority | Issue | Why it is a problem | How to recover it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical | The main experiments use an incorrect graph. | You explicitly state that the graph contains two false borders, Kandy to Ampara and Kegalle to Kalutara, and that the main runs were not repeated after correcting them. A reviewer can immediately ask whether the GNN results are trustworthy. | Correct the adjacency and rerun the main graph experiments. This is probably the single most important thing to do. |
| Critical | Some models were chosen after seeing earlier results. | Six of the 30 main arms were hand-picked after earlier results were known, and the planned 46-arm grid was never completed. You also admit that the BH correction therefore does not represent a clean pre-defined family. | Clearly separate confirmatory experiments from exploratory experiments. Most importantly, clarify whether any test results were seen before choosing those six models. If yes, you need a fresh untouched final test period or new forecast origins. |
| Critical | The experiment needed to answer the title's main question is missing from the strong nine-origin protocol. | Your key question is essentially does SEIR physics add something beyond persistence anchoring? You did compare gated vs gated SEIR, but only in the weaker three-origin experiment. The paper itself says the nine-origin experiment contains no gated head without physics. | On the same encoder, same 9 origins and same seeds, compare: persistence, gated non-SEIR, gated SEIR, bare SEIR. This single clean ablation would make the central conclusion much stronger. |
| Critical | Too many different protocols/datasets are mixed together. | Main results use rebuilt data + 9 origins; some ablations use 3 origins; interval experiments use the legacy dataset containing the backlog week; COVID experiments use "earlier experiment code." Even though you warn readers not to compare them, it makes the evidence feel fragmented. | Make the rebuilt dataset + main rolling protocol the paper's single source of final evidence. Move old legacy experiments to an appendix/history section or rerun the important ones. |
| High | The statistical evidence is weak for small improvements. | There are only 9 paired origins, and you already acknowledge low power for differences around one case. Yet your best gain is only about 1.1 RMSE. | Report paired effect sizes and confidence intervals, not only p-values. If practical, add more temporal origins. Also make clear that "not significant" does not mean "models are equivalent." |
| High | Persistence alone is not enough as the classical baseline set. | Persistence is well motivated and absolutely should stay, but your baseline section mainly compares sophisticated neural models against last-week persistence and a GCN. Dengue is strongly seasonal, so reviewers may ask whether the networks beat simple forecasting models. | Add at least a seasonal naive baseline (same week previous year if data permit) and one simple statistical/autoregressive model such as AR/Poisson/NB regression. This greatly strengthens the paper even if those baselines win. |
| High | The paper over-interprets the 89% correlation. | You state that last week's count explains 89% of current-week variance, then later say the model therefore has "11% of the variance left to explain." That is too strong. A simple lag-1 R^2 does not mean 89% is permanently unavailable to nonlinear/multivariate models. | Change it to something like: "Lag-1 case counts have very high predictive association, leaving limited apparent room for improvement under this forecasting setup." Do not describe 11% as a hard remaining amount. |
| High | "Climate explains only 3%" is also too strong. | The 3% appears to be a squared marginal correlation. That does not show that climate contains only 3% useful predictive information; there could be nonlinear effects, interactions, district-specific effects, or information conditional on cases. | Say "the strongest individual climate variable had r^2 = 0.029", rather than "climate explains only 3%." Your actual lag ablations are better evidence. |
| High | The biological SEIR model is quite simplified, but the title/conclusion sound broad. | You use a human-only SEIR model even though dengue is mosquito-borne, with fixed omega, gamma, reporting fraction 1/11, and simplified initial states. The related-work section itself notes that host-vector SEIR-SEI models are common. | Narrow the claim: this particular SEIR-informed architecture did not provide clear predictive benefit, rather than "epidemic physics does not help." A sensitivity test for rho, omega, gamma would also help. |
| Important | A mathematical statement about the training loss is inaccurate. | The paper says "squared error on log counts targets the conditional median." Strictly, MSE in log space targets E[log(1+Y) given X]. Exponentiating that is not generally the conditional median of Y. | Rewrite this argument. You can simply say that optimizing squared error in transformed log space is not aligned directly with RMSE on the original count scale, motivating the NB experiment. |
| Important | The "reporting backlog" is presented as a fact without enough evidence. | Week 395 has 7165 cases versus about 400 either side, and you "attribute this jump to a reporting backlog." That is plausible, but the paper needs documentary evidence if you call it a backlog. | Cite an epidemiological report confirming it. Otherwise call it an "apparent reporting anomaly consistent with a backlog" and perform the sensitivity analysis exactly as you already do. |
| Important | The results rely too heavily on RMSE alone. | RMSE can be dominated by outbreak weeks/high-count districts. Table II reports useful horizon-wise RMSE, but there is little district-level or alternative-metric evidence. | Add MAE and preferably a skill score relative to persistence / MASE, plus a district-wise summary. You do not need dozens of metrics, 2-3 well-chosen ones are enough. |
| Important | The +/- values in the tables are easy to misinterpret. | You explicitly say the SD over the 27 runs is "dominated by differences between origins." So a reader may mistake it for model training uncertainty. | Report seed variability separately or, preferably, report the distribution/CI of paired model-minus-persistence differences across origins. |
| Important | There are 69 configurations, but the reader cannot reconstruct all of them. | Fig. 3 refers to 69 three-origin configurations and eight comparison families, but only some appear in the tables. | Put the complete 69-model configuration/result table in an appendix or supplementary material. This removes suspicion of selective reporting. |
| Important | Data/covariate provenance needs considerably more detail. | The rebuilt dengue series is explained reasonably well, but climate, policy, workplace mobility, population, missing-value handling, publication delays and exact date ranges are not described at the same level. The main data description mostly focuses on dengue cases. | Add a compact Data Sources table: variable, source, temporal resolution, available dates, lag used, missing-data treatment and whether the value was available at forecast time. |
| Important | Failed STGAT/DCRNN performance needs stronger validation. | STGAT and DCRNN perform extremely badly with direct heads, 62 and 73 RMSE, then suddenly approach persistence after gating. That can make reviewers wonder whether the architectures were implemented/tuned fairly. | Report parameter counts, implementation source, hyperparameters and a sanity/reproduction check. Avoid wording like "STGAT fails" unless you establish that the implementation itself is sound. |

There is also one very important interpretation problem in the abstract. You currently say that learned adjacency, GAN/simulator augmentation, climate lags and COVID policy covariates "gave no measurable test gain." But several of those conclusions come from the three-origin exploratory protocol or older experiment setup, not the same strong nine-origin confirmatory test. I would either rerun the important experiments on the main protocol or explicitly write "exploratory ablations found no clear gain."

The same applies to the interval result in the abstract. The 95% persistence interval result is interesting, but those experiments use the legacy array containing the known anomalous week. I would rerun interval experiments on the rebuilt dataset or remove that result from the abstract and leave it as supplementary analysis.

## The four things I would fix first

If time is limited, the safest recovery path is: first correct the graph and rerun the main experiments; second run the missing gated-vs-gated-SEIR ablation under the full nine-origin protocol; third cleanly separate post-hoc/exploratory models from confirmatory models and protect a genuinely untouched test set; fourth add strong simple baselines such as seasonal naive and a classical autoregressive/count model.

Once those four are fixed, the paper becomes much easier to defend. Right now the paper's most interesting result is actually a good one: under a leakage-controlled evaluation, sophisticated models struggle to beat a very strong persistence baseline. The danger is that the current design lets a reviewer say, "Maybe SEIR did not help because the graph was wrong, the SEIR model was simplified, the decisive ablation is missing, and some models were selected after results were seen." Your next experiments should specifically close those escape routes.

I would also consider narrowing the title from "Does Epidemic Physics Help a Graph Network Forecast Dengue?" to something like "Does SEIR-Informed Graph Learning Improve Weekly Dengue Forecasting? A Leakage-Controlled Evaluation Against Strong Baselines." That matches what you actually tested and makes the negative conclusion much harder to criticize.
