## 1. Continuous-time residual loss

Let the neural network approximate the compartment trajectory and, optionally, unknown parameters:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(t)=\big(\hat S_h,\hat E_h,\hat I_h,\hat R_h,\hat S_v,\hat E_v,\hat I_v\big)(t),
\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\phi}=\text{learned epidemiological parameters}.
$$

For a mechanistic ODE system

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{f}\big(t,\mathbf{x}(t);\boldsymbol\phi\big),
$$

a PINN uses automatic differentiation to calculate $$d\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta/dt$$ and defines the equation residual

$$
\mathbf{r}_\theta(t)
=\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(t)}{dt}
-\mathbf{f}\big(t,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(t);\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}\big).
$$

The standard objective is

$$
\mathcal L(\theta,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi})
=\lambda_{\mathrm{data}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{IC}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{IC}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{phys}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{phys}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}},
$$

with

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{phys}}
=\frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{j=1}^{N_c}
\left\|\mathbf{r}_\theta(t_j^c)\right\|_2^2,
$$

where $$t_j^c$$ are collocation times. The data and initial-condition terms are typically

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}
=\frac{1}{N_d}\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}
\left\|\mathbf{M}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(t_i)-\mathbf{y}_i\right\|_{\mathbf W_y}^2,
$$

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{IC}}
=\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(t_0)-\mathbf{x}_0\right\|_{\mathbf W_0}^2.
$$

Here $$\mathbf M$$ selects observed compartments or outputs, and $$\mathbf W_y$$ can weight observations by their uncertainty. PINNs have been used to learn epidemic parameters—including time-varying contact rates—while enforcing SIR/SEIR dynamics [^1]. The separate data, initial-condition, and residual components need careful balancing; poor weighting can make the network fit observations while violating the ODE, or satisfy the ODE while missing the data [^2].

For a representative dengue SEIR–SEI model, write human compartments as $$S_h,E_h,I_h,R_h$$ and mosquito compartments as $$S_v,E_v,I_v$$. One common normalized form is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot S_h&=\mu_hN_h-\lambda_h S_h-\mu_hS_h,\\
\dot E_h&=\lambda_h S_h-(\sigma_h+\mu_h)E_h,\\
\dot I_h&=\sigma_hE_h-(\gamma_h+\mu_h)I_h,\\
\dot R_h&=\gamma_hI_h-\mu_hR_h,\\
\dot S_v&=\mu_vN_v-\lambda_v S_v-\mu_vS_v,\\
\dot E_v&=\lambda_v S_v-(\sigma_v+\mu_v)E_v,\\
\dot I_v&=\sigma_vE_v-\mu_vI_v,
\end{aligned}
$$

where, for example,

$$
\lambda_h=a b_{vh}\frac{I_v}{N_h},
\qquad
\lambda_v=a b_{hv}\frac{I_h}{N_h}.
$$

The seven scalar residuals are simply each neural derivative minus the corresponding right-hand side. Thus, for example,

$$
 r_{E_h}(t)=\frac{d\hat E_h}{dt}-
\left[\hat\lambda_h\hat S_h-(\hat\sigma_h+\hat\mu_h)\hat E_h\right].
$$

The physics loss is the mean squared sum of all seven residuals, possibly normalized by compartment scales.

## 2. Converting continuous SEIR–SEI equations to a weekly discrete loss

Suppose observations occur at $$t_k=k\Delta t$$ with $$\Delta t=7$$ days. A neural network can still represent a continuous trajectory, but the data term is evaluated only at weekly times:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}^{\mathrm{weekly}}
=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K}
\left\|\mathbf M\hat{\mathbf x}_\theta(t_k)-\mathbf y_k\right\|^2.
$$

There are two distinct cases.

**Weekly state observations.** If $$\mathbf y_k$$ is a measured compartment state or a reconstructed incidence state, enforce the model flow over one week. With forward Euler,

$$
\mathbf r_k^{\mathrm{Euler}}
=\frac{\hat{\mathbf x}_{k+1}-\hat{\mathbf x}_{k}}{\Delta t}
-\mathbf f(t_k,\hat{\mathbf x}_{k};\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}_k).
$$

The discrete physics loss is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{phys}}^{\mathrm{disc}}
=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}
\left\|\mathbf r_k^{\mathrm{Euler}}\right\|^2.
$$

**Weekly reported cases.** Reported cases are usually new infections accumulated over the week, not $$I_h(t_k)$$. If the observation is weekly incidence $$C_k$$, use the model’s incidence flow:

$$
\hat C_k
=\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}}\lambda_h(t)S_h(t)\,dt,
$$

or, under a left-point approximation,

$$
\hat C_k\approx \Delta t\,\lambda_h(t_k)S_h(t_k).
$$

Then

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}^{\mathrm{inc}}
=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}
\left(\hat C_k-C_k\right)^2,
$$

or a count likelihood is preferable, for example

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}^{\mathrm{Pois}}
=-\sum_k\log\operatorname{Poisson}\left(C_k\mid \rho\hat C_k\right),
$$

with $$\rho$$ an ascertainment/reporting factor. This distinction matters: fitting weekly cases directly to the infectious compartment confuses prevalence with incidence.

A higher-accuracy discrete residual uses a one-step numerical flow map $$\Phi_{\Delta t}$$:

$$
\mathbf r_k^{\mathrm{flow}}
=\hat{\mathbf x}_{k+1}
-\Phi_{\Delta t}\left(t_k,\hat{\mathbf x}_k;\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}_k\right),
$$

and

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{phys}}^{\mathrm{flow}}
=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}
\left\|\mathbf r_k^{\mathrm{flow}}\right\|^2.
$$

This formulation is often preferable with weekly data because it enforces the integrated seven-day dynamics rather than a pointwise derivative at a single observation time. It also allows parameters such as transmission rate or mosquito abundance to vary by week, using interpolation or a separate neural parameterization.

## 3. Numerical discretizations used for weekly ODE residuals

**Forward Euler.**

$$
\mathbf x_{k+1}\approx \mathbf x_k+\Delta t\,\mathbf f(t_k,\mathbf x_k).
$$

It is simple and cheap, but its local truncation error is $$O(\Delta t^2)$$ and weekly steps can be too coarse for fast exposed-to-infectious or vector processes. Use it mainly for a baseline or when the weekly step is demonstrably stable.

**Backward Euler / implicit Euler.**

$$
\mathbf x_{k+1}\approx \mathbf x_k+\Delta t\,\mathbf f(t_{k+1},\mathbf x_{k+1}).
$$

This is more stable for stiff SEIR–SEI systems, but the residual is implicit and may require differentiable fixed-point iterations or a differentiable nonlinear solve.

**Trapezoidal rule / Heun.**

$$
\mathbf x_{k+1}\approx \mathbf x_k+\frac{\Delta t}{2}
\left[\mathbf f(t_k,\mathbf x_k)+\mathbf f(t_{k+1},\mathbf x_{k+1})\right].
$$

It is second-order and often a practical compromise for weekly residuals.

**Midpoint Runge–Kutta.**

$$
\mathbf x_{k+1}\approx \mathbf x_k+\Delta t\,\mathbf f\left(t_k+\frac{\Delta t}{2},\mathbf x_k+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf f(t_k,\mathbf x_k)\right).
$$

**Classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf k_1&=\mathbf f(t_k,\mathbf x_k),\\
\mathbf k_2&=\mathbf f(t_k+\Delta t/2,\mathbf x_k+\Delta t\mathbf k_1/2),\\
\mathbf k_3&=\mathbf f(t_k+\Delta t/2,\mathbf x_k+\Delta t\mathbf k_2/2),\\
\mathbf k_4&=\mathbf f(t_k+\Delta t,\mathbf x_k+\Delta t\mathbf k_3),\\
\Phi_{\Delta t}&=\mathbf x_k+\frac{\Delta t}{6}(\mathbf k_1+2\mathbf k_2+2\mathbf k_3+\mathbf k_4).
\end{aligned}
$$

The residual is $$\hat{\mathbf x}_{k+1}-\Phi_{\Delta t}$$. Runge–Kutta schemes are attractive when weekly observations are sparse but the underlying dynamics are nonlinear. Numerical Runge–Kutta integration is also used in epidemiological SIR-plus-recurrent-network work, although that does not by itself imply a PINN residual loss [^3].

**Finite-difference derivative residuals.** If the network is evaluated only at weekly nodes, a centered approximation can be used for interior points:

$$
\mathbf r_k^{\mathrm{CD}}
=\frac{\hat{\mathbf x}_{k+1}-\hat{\mathbf x}_{k-1}}{2\Delta t}
-\mathbf f(t_k,\hat{\mathbf x}_k;\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}_k),
\qquad k=1,\ldots,K-1.
$$

This is second-order accurate for smooth trajectories, but it does not directly enforce the one-week flow and needs one-sided differences at the endpoints. It is useful when the data grid itself is the computational grid; automatic differentiation at additional collocation points is usually more flexible.

**Practical recommendation.** For weekly dengue data, use weekly data/incidence likelihoods plus a flow-map residual based on adaptive RK4/RK45 or an implicit solver, and add interior collocation points if a continuous neural trajectory is desired. Use Euler only after checking stability and conservation. Enforce positivity and population conservation either by a transformed output—e.g., softmax compartments—or by explicit penalties such as

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{cons}}=\sum_k\left(\sum_i\hat x_{i,k}-N\right)^2,
$$

because a low ODE residual alone does not guarantee physically valid compartment values. Epidemiological PINN work emphasizes that these methods can learn disease dynamics and parameters from noisy or incomplete observations [^4][^5], but the exact discretization should be selected from the time scale, stiffness, observation model, and whether the target is prevalence or weekly incidence.
